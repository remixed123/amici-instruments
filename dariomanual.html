1Welcome to Dario

Dario is a live-performance groovebox for iPad that hosts AUv3 plug-ins as its instruments and effects. You bring the plug-ins you already own; Dario gives them a performance surface, a sequencer, a mixer, a modulation rig and a save format — then gets out of the way so you can play them.

Four tracks — which is not a four-part limit

Dario has four tracks. Before that number puts you off: four Dario tracks is not a four-part limit. A track hosts any AUv3, so an eight-part drum machine or a multitimbral workstation takes one of the four and brings every one of its own internal parts with it.

Four tracks is the size of the performance surface — how many things you can grab, mute, fade, roll and mix with your hands — not a cap on how many parts are playing. A four-track setup can easily be twenty parts of music.

On screen the tracks are labelled VOICE 1–VOICE 4, and this guide follows that label. Each is a full track: an instrument slot, two insert effects, its own mixer channel, two sends, its own sequencer lane and its own pair of LFOs.

What a track is depends on what you put in it and how you sequence it:

  • A drum track — a six-part percussion grid sequencer (KICK · SNARE · CH · OH · CLAP · TOM), each sound with its own pattern, generator and density. That is six parts from one track.
  • A monophonic instrument — one note per step, for basslines, acid lines and leads.
  • A polyphonic instrument — a chord per step, for pads, stabs and comping.
  • A multi-part plug-in — an 8-track AUv3 drum machine, a multitimbral synth, a hosted groovebox. It sequences and mixes internally and Dario treats the whole thing as one track.
  • Or a MIDI out to external hardware, sequencing a hardware synth or drum machine that has its own parts again.
  • Or an external instrument — Dario sequences that hardware synth and takes its audio back into the same track, so it runs through Dario's effects, sends and mixer like anything internal.

Four ways to drive a track

Every track picks its own note source, so you can mix approaches freely across one setup:

SourceWhat plays the track
Dario's sequencerThe built-in four-lane algorithmic step sequencer, described below.
A MIDI AUv3A hosted MIDI generator or processor — an arpeggiator, a Euclidean sequencer, a hosted step sequencer — feeding the track's instrument.
External MIDI inA hardware keyboard, a pad controller, an external sequencer, or another app — playing the track live, with full velocity. Set each track to its own MIDI channel and one multitimbral source drives all four independently.
MIDI out to hardwareThe reverse: load MIDI Out into a track and it sequences and controls external gear instead of making sound — a hardware synth, a drum machine or another app. Whatever drives the track goes out to the device, and the mixer faders follow as standard CCs.

A track can also take audio in: load Audio Input to run a live hardware source — a synth, a drum machine, a mic, a modular rig — through the track's effects and mixer, or External Instrument to do both halves at once, MIDI out to the device and its audio back in.

That flexibility is the point. Use your preferred AUv3 instruments, effects, MIDI sequencers and generators; use Dario's own algorithmic sequencer; drive external hardware; or run all of it at once in the same setup.

The algorithmic sequencer

Dario's built-in sequencer is not a piano roll. You choose a rhythm generator and a pitch generator and steer them — Euclidean patterns, probability, random walks, Turing-machine melodies, acid lines, and a generative drum map that drives kick, snare and hat from one X/Y position.

On top of that sits a progression engine. Written in scale degrees rather than fixed notes, a progression moves the harmony through a chord sequence — turning a polyphonic track into a real chord progression and re-rooting a monophonic one to follow the changes. Nine curated genre banks ship, plus a generator that builds an endless supply of coherent progressions.

Everything is snapped to one global Scale and Key, so all four tracks stay in tune with each other no matter how you set them — and a not-sure-where-to-start finder will suggest a scale and key from a genre or a feeling.

Built for playing live

Dario carries the performance features you'd expect on a current hardware groovebox or algorithmic sequencer:

  • SS — Snapshot. Tap it to capture your entire rig, jam as freely and destructively as you like, then tap it again to snap everything back to that moment, instantly and with no audio gap. It captures the internal state of every hosted plug-in too — so you can re-program a hosted sequencer or re-dial a synth patch mid-jam and still get back. See 13.5.
  • MB — Master Button. Arm it and your button presses queue instead of firing — mutes, fades, solos, rolls and mapped toggles all flash to show they're waiting. Tap MB again and every queued change commits at the same instant, so you can drop three parts out and launch a fill on the beat rather than chasing them one tap at a time. See 13.4.
  • Live automation recording. Hit Record while the transport runs and move anything — a plug-in parameter, a send, a mixer fader. The movement is captured over the loop and plays back on the next pass. Touch the control again and you overdub just that one, leaving the rest intact. See 12.
  • FADE and ROLL per track — a tempo-locked fade that ends in a mute, and a latched fill you hand back to the pattern in time.
  • A performance lock that blocks whichever actions you'd hate to trigger by accident mid-set, while leaving everything else playable.

Ten LFOs add hands-off movement, and the whole rig — every plug-in and its own internal sound — saves as a Groovebox you can reload or share as a single file.

Modulation and sidechain, built in

Ten LFOs — two per track plus two general-purpose — each driving up to six destinations at once. As well as any hosted plug-in's parameters, they reach Dario's own mixer, its sequencer generators so patterns rewrite themselves bar by bar, and MIDI out to external gear. See 9. MODULATE Tab.

An integrated sidechain ducker gives you the pumping duck of a pad against a kick with no compressor to load or trigger bus to route — and its envelope is itself modulatable and playable from a slider. See 6.4 Sidechain.

It all lands on the PERFORM tab

This is what ties the app together. Every voice and FX card carries generic sliders and buttons, and you long-press any of them to assign it — to three destinations at once:

  • A plug-in parameter — any published parameter of any plug-in in that section, two-way, at full resolution.
  • A built-in target — arm or sweep an LFO, steer a sequencer generator, or ride the sidechain envelope.
  • A MIDI message out to external hardware.

So a single slider can open a synth's filter, speed up an LFO, nudge a sequencer parameter and send a CC to a hardware synth in one gesture. A button set to an LFO's on/off drops modulation in and out without leaving the tab.

Everything else you need mid-set is already there or one strip away: the mixer, mute, solo, FADE, ROLL, the transport, SS, MB and the sidechain all sit on PERFORM or in the bottom strip that stays visible on every tab. MIDI Learn then maps your hardware controller onto any of it.

The point of all this: you shouldn't need to go menu-diving during a set. Build the rig across SEQUENCE and MODULATE, map what matters onto PERFORM's sliders and buttons, and play the whole thing — plug-ins, sequencer, modulation, sidechain and external gear — from one screen.

This guide covers every panel and control. If you're new to Dario, read section 3 and then work through the tabs. If you're after one feature, use the table of contents on the left.

Dario, Bruno and Marco are family. Dario shares its visual language with the other Amici instruments, and its two built-in instruments are built from their engines — the Dario Drum Kit reuses Bruno's analog drum engines, and the Dario Poly Synth reuses Marco's synthesis blocks. You don't need either app to use them; they're included.
You don't need to buy more plug-ins. Dario works with the AUv3s you already own, and ships with a drum kit, a polysynth, an effects chain and a MIDI monitor of its own. The Factory and Artist Grooveboxes are there to show what's possible, not to sell you a shopping list.

Dario is an iPad-only app. It runs in landscape and requires iPadOS 18.6 or later.

That system requirement — not the app itself — sets the floor for which iPads are supported: any iPad that can install iPadOS 18.6 can run Dario.

"Supported" and "runs comfortably" are not the same thing

Dario is an out-of-process AUv3 host: it runs several third-party plug-ins as voices and effects, sequences and mixes them, and drives live modulation on top. That workload is far heavier than a typical app.

  • The 2018 iPad Pro (A12X) is the oldest supported device but, thanks to its Pro-class chip, handles Dario's plug-in load well.
  • The iPad (7th gen, A10 Fusion, 3 GB RAM) is newer but has the weakest chip and least memory of any supported model — so it, not the 2018 Pro, is the real performance floor. Keep voice and effect counts modest on it.

As a rule of thumb, an A12-class chip or newer with at least 3 GB of RAM is the practical minimum for a full multi-voice Groovebox. More capable iPads leave more headroom for dense setups and heavier plug-ins.

Window scaling

Dario's interface is a fixed landscape control surface designed at a floor size of 1000 × 720 pt. At that size or larger — any supported iPad full-screen — the layout fills the window normally. Make the window smaller, in Split View or Stage Manager, and the whole surface shrinks uniformly to fit rather than reflowing: text, sliders, buttons and spacing all scale down together. It is always on; there is no setting.

3Getting Started

The quickest way to understand Dario is to load a ready-made Groovebox and pull it apart.

  1. Open Dario. Tap the Groovebox pill in the master strip.
  2. Pick a Factory or Artist groovebox. The details panel on the right lists the plug-ins it needs and flags any you don't have installed. Tap Load Groovebox.
  3. Press Play (the wide button at the bottom, under the effects column). You now have a running groove.
  4. Grab the P1–P8 sliders on any voice. They're already mapped to that plug-in's parameters — move them and listen.
  5. Go to the SEQUENCE tab. Pick a voice's lane, tap ALGO on the STEPS page, and try a different rhythm generator. Then tap a few cells on the grid by hand.
  6. Go to the MODULATE tab, turn an LFO ON, and watch the control it's driving move on its own.

Starting from nothing

To build your own rig instead, load the Blank groovebox from the Factory category, then:

  1. Tap VOICE 1's top (instrument) slot. The picker opens with Dario's own instruments pinned at the top — Dario Drum Kit, Dario Poly Synth, MIDI Out, Audio Input and External Instrument — above every AUv3 instrument on your iPad. Pick one.
  2. Go to SEQUENCE, leave the lane on SEQ, choose DRUMS for a kit or MONO for a melodic part, and tap some steps in.
  3. Press Play.
  4. Back on PERFORM, long-press a P-slider to map it onto a parameter of the plug-in you loaded (see 11. Assigning Controls).
  5. Repeat for voices 2–4, set the Scale and BPM in the master strip, then save with Save As in the Groovebox browser.
Set the Scale and Key early. It's global, and every melodic lane snaps to it — so getting it right up front means the four voices are in tune with each other from the first note you sequence. If you're not sure what to choose, the Scale pill's GENRE / EMOTION tab will suggest one.

5Working with Plug-ins

Everything that makes sound in Dario is a plug-in in a slot — whether it's a third-party AUv3 you own or one of Dario's own built-ins.

Loading, opening, bypassing, removing

Every slot behaves the same way:

  • Tap an empty slot to open the picker for that slot's kind — instruments for a voice's source slot, effects for an insert or bus slot, MIDI plug-ins for the SEQUENCE tab's processor slots.
  • Tap a loaded slot to open the plug-in's own window.
  • Slide up on a loaded slot to bypass it (a power badge appears).
  • Slide down to remove it.

Dario's own plug-ins are pinned at the top of each picker, above the third-party list.

Plug-in windows

Hosted plug-ins open in floating windows you can drag and resize. Three settings under Menu → AUv3 Window Settings control them:

  • When Opening — how a plug-in window presents when you tap its slot.
  • Maximised Area — how much of the screen a maximised window fills: Show AUv3s, Large View or Full Screen.
  • Open Windows — a cap on how many plug-in windows can be open at once.
Open Windows is a resource dial. Every open window is live and drawing, so the cap is what keeps a rig with many plug-ins responsive. There's no dormant-placeholder state — a window is either open and running or closed.

Hosted plug-in windows keep a neutral dark frame on every theme, and the plug-in's own interface always draws in its own colours — Dario can't restyle a third-party plug-in.

Out-of-process hosting

Dario hosts AUv3s out of process, the same way AUM and Drambo do. Practically, that means a badly-behaved plug-in can crash without taking Dario down with it: the slot goes quiet, the rest of your rig keeps playing, and you can reload it.

Hardware audio input

A voice's source slot can host Audio Input instead of an instrument, bringing a live hardware input into that voice's channel — through its inserts, its sends, its sidechain and the mixer like any other voice. External Instrument goes a step further: it sends the voice's notes out to a hardware synth and returns that synth's audio into the same voice. See 14.5 and 14.6.

One input device, shared by every input voice. iOS binds a single hardware input for the whole app, so the device is chosen once in Menu → Audio Engine → Audio Input, not per voice. Each voice still picks its own channels from that device, so a multi-channel interface can feed several voices at once. Device and channels are both saved with the Groovebox.

6.2 The Effects Column

To the right of the voices, three cards:

  • FX 1 and FX 2 — shared aux send buses. Each hosts three effect slots, four assignable P1–P4 sliders and four buttons. Voices feed them through their FX 1 / FX 2 send faders and the processed signal returns to the mix — ideal for a shared reverb or delay.
  • MASTER — the master insert chain: three effect slots processing the whole mix before the gain stage.
An empty FX bus is silent. A bus with no effect loaded isn't send-filled, rendered or summed, so a voice's send does nothing until you load something into it. A send to an empty bus is not a dry pass-through.

The MASTER section is a fixed part of the mixer and can't be renamed.

6.3 Mixer & Master

Per voice: Vol, Pan, and the two FX sends, plus MUTE and SOLO in the bottom strip.

On the right edge, the master column stacks PAN (bipolar, up = Right, with a centre detent) over GAIN (0–200 %, boosting to +12 dB), then the SC and CLR buttons.

All of the per-voice mixer faders — and any mapped slider or button — can be recorded as live automation.

The master GAIN is deliberately not automatable. It's a global output level, kept out of the record system entirely — so a recorded pass can never wind your whole output up or down behind your back. It can still be MIDI-learned.

6.5 Custom Names

Give a voice or an FX bus a name that fits your kit — DRUM KIT, LEAD, BASS, REVERB — instead of the default VOICE 1–4 / FX 1–2.

How Euclidean notes work

EUCLID is the most conceptual of the pitch generators, but the idea is simple. The STEPS-page EUCLID spreads a number of hits evenly in time. The NOTES-page EUCLID does the same trick with pitch: it takes the notes of the current Scale and Key and picks a handful spread evenly across the scale, giving a balanced, musical pool rather than a random scatter. Because every pick is a scale note, a EUCLID line is always in key.

Think of it as: rhythm-EUCLID = "when does a note happen?" and notes-EUCLID = "which notes are chosen from the scale?" The two are completely independent — run a Euclidean rhythm on STEPS and a Euclidean pitch pool on NOTES at the same time.
SliderWhat it does
COUNTHow many notes end up in the pool. Low counts give sparse, riff-like material (2–3 notes is roughly an arpeggio cell); higher counts fill more of the scale in.
ROTATESlides the even selection along the scale, giving a different set of the same size. The quickest way to audition variations.
SPANHow many octaves the pool spreads over (1–3 OCT). SPAN 1 keeps everything in one octave; SPAN 2–3 lets the pool leap across octaves for wider lines. COUNT's ceiling grows with SPAN, since there are more positions to choose from.

A worked example. Key C Major, COUNT 3, ROTATE 0, SPAN 1. The scale has seven notes (C D E F G A B); EUCLID spreads three picks evenly across them and lands on C · E · G. Those are laid across the 16 steps in order and repeat. Nudge ROTATE and you get a different evenly-spread trio of the same size. Raise SPAN to 2 and the same three picks are chosen from fourteen positions across two octaves, so the line opens up.

EUCLID only decides which notes are available. Everything else still applies: PLAY DIR sets the order the pool is read, PROG moves the whole pool through a chord sequence, and POLY + CHORD voices each pool note as a chord.

Use your ears, not the maths. Set the lane to MONO, choose EUCLID, and turn COUNT and ROTATE until a phrase catches your ear — then shape it with PLAY DIR, OCTAVE and GATE. Because it's fully deterministic, anything you find is repeatable and saved with the Groovebox.

Buttons (NOTES)

  • ARP — an arpeggiator overlay that layers on top of whatever note source is chosen; it does not replace it. OFF · UP · DOWN · UP-DN · DN-UP · RANDOM. When on, the note source becomes the arp's moving root and the CHORD's tones are cycled across the steps — so you can arpeggiate a MANUAL line, a WALK or a TURING line. In MONO it's a classic single-note arpeggio; in POLY it voices each arp note as a chord. Its slider is OCTS (1–4).
  • CHORD — shown when the lane is POLY or ARP is on. The chord shape built on each root: the diatonic voicings DIA (triad), DIA7 (seventh) and DIA9 (ninth), which are always in key with the quality following the scale; plus the fixed "planing" shapes MAJ · MIN · MAJ7 · MIN7 · DOM7 · SUS2 · SUS4 · 5TH.
  • PROG — a harmonic-progression overlay that moves the root through a chord sequence, one chord per loop. In POLY it becomes a chord progression; in MONO the melodic line keeps its shape but is re-rooted to each chord. See below.
  • INV — POLY chord inversion: ROOT · 1ST · 2ND · 3RD, lifting the lowest chord tones an octave. Same notes, different voicing and bass note — handy for smoother voice-leading through a progression. Disabled on a MONO lane.
  • OCTAVE — shifts the whole lane by −4…+4 octaves.
  • TIE — a per-step editor: mark a step to tie into the next note. See 7.5.
  • GATE — always present on NOTES. Note length as a percentage of the note's slot (up to the next played step); the top notch is HOLD, which rings until the next note.

PROG — choosing a progression

Every progression is written in scale degrees, so it stays correct in any Key and Scale. The choices are:

  • OFF — no progression; the part stays on the tonic.

7.5 Tie (Legato)

A tied step holds through into the next note as one legato gesture — the next onset doesn't retrigger the envelope, so notes join smoothly instead of re-plucking. Put TIE on the step before the one you want it to join into.

Reliable on any synth, with no setup. TIE is pure articulation: Dario holds the note and plays the next one legato. It sends no portamento or glide control messages, so there's nothing the hosted plug-in has to be configured for — it works whether the synth is mono or poly.

It is not a pitch glide. TIE joins notes; it doesn't swoop between pitches. For a 303-style glide, use the hosted synth's own portamento — set it up inside the plug-in and it will glide across the tied notes. Dario deliberately doesn't try to drive glide over MIDI, because it can't do so reliably across arbitrary plug-ins.

7.7 Modulating the Generators

You can drive the generator sliders with an LFO, from the MODULATE tab's Sequencer targets, or by hand from a PERFORM slider (see 11.2).

Because these sliders re-make the whole pattern, a change to one lands at the start of the next bar, not the instant it moves. A slow LFO on EUCLID PULSES, WALK RANGE, TURING CHANGE, TECHNO JUMP or notes-EUCLID COUNT / ROTATE steps the pattern cleanly from one bar to the next instead of jumping mid-phrase. The bar you're hearing always plays out whole.

Two controls are deliberately immediate, moving smoothly through the bar, because they shape feel rather than rebuild the pattern:

  • GATE — note length. Sweep it for breathing, gating rhythms.
  • CHANCE (PROB) — the per-step fire probability. It already re-rolls every bar, so modulating it live keeps the variation flowing.

For a strictly in-time feel on those two, run the LFO in SYNC mode. Manual moves are always immediate: drag a generator slider or tap steps on the grid and you hear it straight away.

A slow LFO on notes-EUCLID ROTATE is a lovely way to let a Euclidean pool drift through the scale, bar by bar — the phrase keeps its shape and density while the actual notes move underneath it.

8How Melodic Sequencing Works

This section explains how Dario turns your settings into actual notes on a MONO or POLY lane, and why the results stay in tune. If you don't know music theory, you can still follow what each control does and why combining them works. If you do, you can confirm exactly how Dario voices notes, builds chords and moves progressions.

For the control-by-control reference, see 7.4. This is about the musical model behind it.

The big idea

Dario separates a melodic part into three independent questions and answers them in a fixed order:

QuestionWhere it's answeredWhat it decides
When?STEPS pageWhich steps fire, and how they play — rhythm and groove.
What pitch?NOTES pageWhich note each firing step plays, and how it moves over time.
How is it voiced?MONO / POLYA single note, or a chord — plus its inversion.

Then everything is forced into the global Scale and Key before it sounds. That's the safety net: nothing is ever out of key.

Theory note. This is the classic separation of rhythm, melody and harmony into orthogonal layers. Because pitch is always quantised to a single global diatonic collection, the harmonic field is fixed and consonant by construction; musical interest then comes from rhythm, register, density and harmonic motion rather than from chromatic pitch choice.

8.1 Scale + Key — the Shared Palette

Everything starts here. You pick a Key (the root note, C…B) and a Scale in the master strip. Together they define a small set of allowed notes — the palette every lane paints from.

  • Every per-step pitch, every chord tone and every generated line is snapped to the nearest note in that scale before it plays. If a calculation lands between two scale notes, Dario rounds to the closest; ties round down, the usual convention.
  • Because the Key and Scale are global, all four voices always share the same palette. Two lanes can play completely different rhythms and melodies and still never clash — they're drawing from the same in-tune set of notes.
  • Pick Chromatic if you want no snapping at all.
Theory note. The snap is a nearest-neighbour quantiser over a 12-bit scale mask (bit n = semitone n above the root is in-scale). That's why lanes are guaranteed consonant with one another: they are all projected onto the same pitch-class set.

8.2 MONO — One Note per Step

On a MONO lane, each firing step plays exactly one note. The pipeline for a single step:

  1. The note ALGO picks a pitch offset for this step — the value you drew in MANUAL, or a generated offset in semitones from the base note.
  2. PROG shifts it, if a progression is on: the current chord's root (a scale degree) is added, so the whole line is re-rooted to that chord for this bar.
  3. ARP overlays, if ARP is on: the CHORD's tones are cycled across the steps on top of the moving root, giving a classic arpeggio.
  4. Snap to the global Scale and Key — and that's the note that plays.

GATE, velocity, TIE and RATCHET then shape how long it lasts, how hard it hits, and how it joins the next note.

So on a MONO lane a progression re-roots your melody: the shape of the line stays the same, but each bar it's transposed to sit on the next chord's root — a moving bassline or a lead that follows the changes.

8.3 POLY — A Chord per Step

On a POLY lane, each firing step plays a chord: a root note plus the notes stacked on top by the CHORD setting.

Steps 1–3 are identical to MONO — they produce the chord's root for this step. Then:

  1. CHORD builds the stack on that root.
    • Diatonic (DIA / DIA7 / DIA9) stacks notes by scale degree — root, 3rd, 5th and so on — so the chord quality (major, minor, diminished) is chosen automatically to fit the key. Always in key.
    • Fixed "planing" shapes (MAJ, MIN, MAJ7, SUS4, 5TH…) stack fixed intervals, giving the same shape on every root. These can sit outside the key — a deliberate colour.
  2. INV applies the inversion — lifting the lowest tones an octave for the same chord with a different bass note, and smoother voice-leading.

The key thing: with the diatonic shapes, Dario chooses each chord's quality for you from the scale. On the second degree of a major scale it builds a minor chord, on the fifth a major chord — exactly what a musician would do by hand.

Theory note. DIA stacks scale degrees (0·2·4) so the triad quality is derived from the scale rather than imposed — true diatonic triads, sevenths and ninths (DIA7 = 0·2·4·6; DIA9 = 0·2·6·8, i.e. root·3rd·7th·9th with the 5th omitted). The fixed shapes are planing — parallel voicings of a constant interval set — and are intentionally allowed to leave the key. When a progression is active only the diatonic shapes are offered, guaranteeing functional, in-key harmony.

8.4 Progressions

A progression is what makes a part go somewhere: instead of sitting on one chord, the harmony moves through a sequence like i → VII → VI.

  • A progression is written in scale degrees (roman numerals), not fixed notes. Degree 0 is the tonic — "home" — degree 4 is the fifth, and so on. Because it's stored as degrees, the same progression works in any Key and any Scale: change the global Key and the whole progression transposes with it, still correct.
  • It advances one chord per loop of the lane.
  • In POLY it's a chord progression, each chord voiced diatonically. In MONO it re-roots the melody to each chord in turn.

Theory note — GEN is functional harmony. GEN walks the standard functional groups — Tonic {I, vi, iii} → Pre-dominant {IV, ii} → Dominant {V, vii°} → back to Tonic — with weighted transitions and a cadence on the last chord. This keeps "ii–V–I" meaning the functional ii, V and I even on a five- or six-note scale; the resulting chord tones are then snapped back into the actual scale. Without it, degree numbers would index the wrong notes.

8.5 Harmonic Rhythm & Layering

A progression advances once per loop of the lane — and how long a loop is depends on STEPS-page settings:

  • LENGTH — a shorter lane means a shorter loop, so the progression changes chord more often.
  • DIV/MULT — a faster lane completes its loop sooner, so the progression moves faster; a slower lane holds each chord longer.

This is the crucial connection between the two pages: the STEPS page controls the harmonic rhythm — how fast the chords change — while PROG controls which chords.

Two voices can share the same Scale, the same Key and even the same progression, yet sound completely different and still musically related, simply because their STEPS settings differ:

  • Give a pad a 16-step ×1 loop and a bass an 8-step ×1 loop on the same progression, and the bass moves through the changes twice for every once of the pad — locked together but interlocking rhythmically.
  • Give two voices different LENGTHs — say 16 and 12 — and their loops drift in and out of phase over time, endlessly recombining, but never dissonantly, because both are in the same key.
  • Keep everything else the same and change one voice's rhythm — EUCLID pulses, PROB density, a different PLAY DIR — and you get a variation on the same harmony: a counter-melody that's guaranteed to fit.
Why this works. Sharing one global Scale and Key fixes the harmonic field, so any combination of notes drawn from it is consonant. Varying only the rhythm over that shared field is exactly how counterpoint, arrangement and layering work — independent rhythmic lines built from the same pitch collection are designed to combine. Different LENGTH and DIV values create polymeter and phasing, a staple of techno, house and minimalist music. The consonance is guaranteed by the shared scale; the interest comes from the differing rhythms and loop lengths.

Independent by default

Each voice runs and advances its own progression on its own loop. So two voices with different progressions are two independent — but always in-key — harmonies layered together: lush and consonant, but not spelling one shared chord at the same time. In electronic music that's often exactly what you want, like a static vamp under an evolving pad.

To lock voices to the same changes — pads, bassline and lead all following one progression — give each voice the same progression (same style and PRESET, or the same GEN VAR / LEN / COLOR) and the same LENGTH and DIV/MULT so their loops line up bar for bar. Keep the POLY voice's CHORD on DIA for correct in-key chord qualities.

8.6 A Worked Example

Global: Key A, Scale Natural Minor.

  • Voice 1 (pad), POLY — PROG = D&B i–VI–VII, CHORD = DIA7, LENGTH 16, ×1.
    Each bar plays a lush diatonic seventh chord: Am7 → Fmaj7 → G7, repeating. The qualities are chosen automatically from the scale — the i is minor, the VI a major seventh, the VII a dominant seventh — all in key, with no chord types to pick by hand.
  • Voice 2 (bass), MONO — the same PROG i–VI–VII, note ALGO = MANUAL (a simple rhythmic root pattern), LENGTH 8, ×1.
    The bass plays your pattern re-rooted to A, then F, then G — and because its loop is 8 steps, it moves through the changes twice as often as the pad, interlocking with it.
  • Voice 3 (lead), MONO — no PROG, note ALGO = EUCLID (COUNT 3), PLAY DIR PING PONG.
    A repeating in-key melodic cell floating over the moving chords. It shares the key, so it consonantly fits every chord in the progression without being told about them.

Three parts, one palette, different rhythms and loop lengths — related, evolving, and never out of tune.

9.4 How LFOs Behave

  • Transport-locked. Every LFO advances only while the transport plays, holds still when stopped, and retriggers to its PHASE on Play — aligned to the shared downbeat under Link, the external Start under MIDI Clock In, or the first beat under the internal clock. SYNC only chooses whether the rate is a tempo division or a fixed Hz; both retrigger the same way.
  • GATE adds per-note key-sync (voice LFOs). With GATE on, the LFO also retriggers to its PHASE on every note the voice's built-in step sequencer plays — a filter sweep or tremolo that restarts on each hit. Notes from a hosted MIDI generator or external MIDI in don't trigger it.
  • They drive the real control. A mixer target moves the voice's actual PERFORM slider; a plug-in-parameter target moves that parameter, and any PERFORM slider mapped to it follows and locks.
  • Sequencer targets change on the bar — see 7.7. GATE and CHANCE are the exceptions and move smoothly.
  • Colour follows the theme — voice LFOs take the voice colour, LFO A / B the distinct LFO accent.
Why transport-locked? It guarantees that a sound you tweaked live reproduces identically when you save the Groovebox and reload it, rather than depending on where a free-running LFO happened to be at the time.
Drive LFOs from PERFORM too. The reverse works: a PERFORM slider or button can arm an LFO, sweep its rate, or change its range — see 11.2. A button mapped to an LFO's ON / OFF is the quickest way to drop modulation in and out during a performance.

Send a MIDI message to external gear as you move the control. It uses that voice's MIDI Out destination and channel, so it goes out when the voice's instrument is the built-in MIDI Out.

12Live Automation

Dario records control movements as you perform and plays them back in time with the sequencer — a live tool for jamming. Move a filter slider, a send, a mixer fader or a momentary button while recording, and that motion loops.

Recording

  1. Press Play. Automation is a live tool — you can only record while playing.
  2. Press Record (the red button in the bottom strip). It arms while playing; stopping playback disarms it.
  3. Move a control. Its movement is captured over the length of the loop. On the next pass it plays back — the control moves on its own, marked with a small red dot.

You can keep refining. Touching a control again re-records only that control, for the part of the loop you move through — an overdub, with everything else you've recorded kept. Grabbing an automated control while playing also lets you take over manually: it stops following its automation until you let go.

What can be automated

  • Any slider or button mapped to a plug-in parameter.
  • A slider that only sends MIDI — a CC, pitch bend, pressure or program sweep to MIDI Out, with no plug-in mapping. Its movement is recorded and replays the MIDI on playback.
  • The dedicated per-voice mixer faders: each voice's Vol, Pan, and FX 1 / FX 2 sends.
  • Buttons in Momentary mode, recorded as a gate — engaged while you held it.

The master GAIN is deliberately not automatable — it's a global output level, kept off the record system. (Toggle-button automation is not recorded yet.)

Clearing automation — the CLR button

Clearing is done with the CLR button in the right-edge master column. CLR is a toggle: tap to arm it (it lights up), tap again to disarm.

  • One control at a time — arm CLR, then tap any automated control to erase just its automation. Controls holding automation show a red eraser, and while CLR is armed they're temporarily locked from moving so you can't nudge one by mistake.
  • Everything on the tab — hold CLR to clear all automation on the PERFORM tab at once.

You can also long-press a single slider or button and choose Clear Automation from its config dialog.

13Live Performance Tools

A set of hands-on tools for playing Dario live, all reachable from the always-visible bottom strip and the VOICE headers. They let you make big, timed and grouped moves — and take a safety net you can jump back to.

13.1 FADE — Timed Fade to Mute

Each voice's FADE button (between MUTE and SOLO) performs a smooth, tempo-locked volume fade that finishes by flipping the voice's mute:

  • If the voice is playing, FADE fades it down to silence over a set number of sequence loops, then switches MUTE on.
  • If the voice is muted, FADE unmutes it and fades it up from silence to its normal level over the same number of loops.

While a fade runs the FADE button stays lit. The fade rides on top of the volume fader — it never moves your Vol slider, so when a fade-out completes the voice simply sits muted at its normal level, ready to bring back.

  • FADE follows the transport, so it only acts while Play is running. Pressing it while stopped does nothing — there are no loops to fade across.
  • Press FADE again while a fade is in progress to cancel it. The fade stops where it is and the mute state is left unchanged.

13.2 ROLL — Live Fill / Retrigger

Each voice's ROLL button (right of SOLO) is a latch: tap it to take over the lane and hammer the note the voice is currently on at a set subdivision, and tap again to release it straight back to the running pattern, in time.

  1. Set the voice's roll up once from its VOICE header cog.
  2. Tap ROLL to latch it on. The button stays lit while it rolls. Tap again to drop back to the normal pattern.

The roll freezes the note the lane is on at the moment you tap, and repeats that note for as long as the latch is held — a roll is one note hammered. You can instead pin a fixed note in the cog's ROLL → NOTE setting.

Note sourceWhat ROLL does
SEQReplaces the pattern for that sound. In DRUMS mode the pinned note selects which drum sound rolls — that sound's lane hammers while the rest of the kit keeps playing.

  • LAUNCH/STOP quantises both ends. OFF starts and stops instantly; a grid setting waits for the next boundary on the way in and on the way out, so the roll and its hand-back both lock up tight. With a grid setting the button stays lit until the roll actually hands back.
  • Rolls are anchored to the transport's clock, so hits stay in time while held.
  • Each voice has its own roll settings — latch a fast 1/32 snare buzz on one and a bar-building fill on another.
  • The latch stays on until you tap it off. It does not stop by itself, so remember to release it.

13.4 MB — Master Button

MB (right of Record) lets you line up several button presses and commit them at the same instant — ideal for dropping multiple voices in or out on the beat.

  1. Tap MB to arm it. It lights up and flashes.
  2. Press any of the batchable controls. They don't act yet; instead they flash to show they're queued:
    • Per-voice MUTE, FADE, SOLO and ROLL — so you can drop a voice out and kick off a fill on another in the same instant.
    • Assignable B1–B6 buttons that are mapped to a plug-in parameter and set to Toggle.
  3. Tap a flashing control again to remove it from the queue.
  4. Tap MB again to commit. Every queued press happens together, and MB disarms.

What isn't batched: CLR acts immediately, as always. Unmapped buttons act immediately too — they just toggle their light. Momentary buttons do nothing while MB is armed, since batching only makes sense for on/off style controls.

13.5 SS — Snapshot

SS (left of MB) is a one-button safety net for improvising.

  1. Tap SS to take a snapshot of your whole setup. It lights up and flashes.
  2. Jam. Change anything you like across PERFORM, SEQUENCE and MODULATE — mixer levels, pans and sends, mutes and solos, plug-in knobs, sequence steps, LFOs, tempo.
  3. Tap SS again to instantly snap the whole rig back to the moment you captured. SS turns off; tap again any time to grab a fresh snapshot.

  • Snapshot reverts settings and plug-in parameters, but leaves your plug-in line-up as-is. Swap or remove a plug-in during the jam and SS won't bring the old one back; slots that still hold the same plug-in do snap their knobs back.
  • The snapshot lives only for the current session and isn't saved into the Groovebox. Loading or resetting clears any held snapshot.
  • The Performance Lock is deliberately not part of a snapshot — it's a safety control, not a musical setting, so snapping back never changes whether you're locked.

Active-state flashing

The three transport-row toggles that put the app into a live or armed state — Record, SS and MB — flash while they're on, so it's obvious at a glance that something is engaged. Record flashes while recording automation; SS while a snapshot is held; MB while the latch is armed waiting for you to commit.

The flash is a hard blink about three times a second, not a soft glow — it's meant to catch your eye across the room. (That's separate from the gentler pulse a queued control shows while MB is armed.)

Open its window to choose the Destination and Channel.

Destination

The menu lists every external MIDI input Dario can see — other apps and hardware connected by USB, Bluetooth or network — plus None. A destination is remembered by the device itself, so a saved Groovebox reconnects to the same hardware next time. If the device isn't connected when you open the Groovebox, its name shows with (offline) and reconnects once it reappears.

Channel

  • Pass-through — each note is sent on the channel it arrives on. This preserves a multitimbral or per-lane stream unchanged: the lane's STEPS CHANNEL, the DRUMS grid's per-sound channels, or an AUv3 generator that already sets channels. Drive a multitimbral external drum machine this way.
  • 1–16 — remaps every note to that one fixed channel. Use this to drive a single hardware synth listening on one channel.

Per-voice control messages have no single note channel, so in Pass-through they go out on channel 1; on a fixed channel they use that channel.

What gets sent

  • The notes that reach the instrument, at the pitch, velocity and timing they're generated — from any of the voice's note sources, with no per-mode difference. Notes are stamped sample-accurately within the audio buffer, so fast arps and sequences keep their timing on the external device.
  • Note-on and note-off, plus CC, pitch bend, program change and aftertouch for sources that emit them.

The input device is global

iOS binds one hardware input for the whole app, so the device is chosen once, in Menu → Audio Engine → Audio Input — not per voice. Every Audio Input and External Instrument voice shares it; only the channels are per voice. Each voice's own window shows the device as a read-only label, with a reminder of where to change it.

MONO or STEREO

The MODE switch in the voice's window decides what the channel grid offers:

  • MONO — individual channels (In 1, In 2 … In 16). Tick any number of them; every ticked channel is summed equally into the voice. Tick one for a single mono source, or several to sum them.
  • STEREO — stereo pairs (In 1 and In 2, In 3 and In 4 …). The pair's first channel goes to Left, its second to Right; tick more than one pair and they sum per side.

Switching MODE clears the current selection — a set of mono channels isn't the same as a set of pairs — so re-pick after you flip it. ALL ticks everything, NONE clears. An empty selection is allowed; the voice is simply silent until you pick a channel.

More than one input voice at once

A multi-channel interface (say an Expert Sleepers ES-9 with 16 inputs) exposes all of its channels, so you can feed different channels to different voices — VOICE 1 on STEREO In 1 and In 2, VOICE 2 on In 3 and In 4, VOICE 3 on MONO In 5. They all run at once off the one interface, each with its own effects, sends and mixer strip.

One device in, a different one out. iOS uses a single device at a time, but with two interfaces connected it can often take audio in from one — a USB synth, say — while your main interface stays the output. Whether a given pair can be split is up to iOS: some pairs stay independent, others get aggregated into one device that can't be split. If iOS won't honour your choice, Dario shows an orange warning naming the device it's actually using. Because both Audio Output and Audio Input are settable in Audio Engine settings, the combination you want is often still reachable — swap the order you plug the USB cables in, then set output and input again. Failing that, patch the synth into your main interface's inputs instead.
It's saved with the Groovebox. The input device (remembered by the device itself, not its position in a list) plus each voice's channels and MONO/STEREO all come back when you reopen the Groovebox. If that interface isn't connected, an orange note says so in both the voice's window and Audio Engine settings — reconnect it, or pick another input. An interface that enumerates a moment after the Groovebox opens is picked up automatically, so the voice comes up playing without you opening its window.

15Grooveboxes

A Groovebox is Dario's save unit — a whole setup you build and store: every loaded AUv3 and its own sound, all slider and button assignments and their automation, the mixer, the sequences, and the transport and master settings.

Think of a Drambo project or an AUM session, but the thing you save is a Groovebox.

15.1 The Browser

Tap the Groovebox pill in the master strip. The browser is a fly-out in three columns:

  • CATEGORY (left) — Factory, Artist, Developer, User.
  • GROOVEBOX (middle) — the grooveboxes in the selected category. Each row shows its name, how many plug-ins it uses, and when it was last saved.

Deleting

Select one of your User grooveboxes and tap the trash button in the details panel. A confirmation asks before it's permanently deleted — this can't be undone. Only User grooveboxes can be deleted.

15.4 Export & Import

A saved Groovebox carries its whole sound — every plug-in plus its own settings — so you can share a complete, ready-to-play setup with someone else.

  • Export. Select any Groovebox and tap the share button in the details panel. Dario shares it as a .dario file you can send by AirDrop, Messages or Mail, or save into Files.
  • Import. Tap IMPORT in the browser header to pick a .dario (or .json) file from Files or another app. It's added to your User category under a unique name — it never overwrites one you already have — and selected so you can review and Load it. You can also just open a .dario file from Files, AirDrop or Mail and Dario imports it and opens it straight away.

Missing plug-ins carry over. A shared Groovebox still lists the AUv3s it needs. If you don't own one, its slot loads empty and stays flagged with a Get link, exactly like any other Groovebox.

A note on samples. Most plug-ins store their full sound in the Groovebox. A few sampler-style plug-ins keep their samples as references to files on the device that made them; those references may not resolve on another iPad, so the plug-in loads but its samples might not. This depends on the plug-in, not on Dario.

16MIDI Control (MIDI Learn)

Drive Dario's on-screen controls from an external MIDI controller. MIDI Learn maps a controller's knob, fader or pad onto almost any Dario control, so you can play the whole rig hands-on from hardware.

Dario is primarily a touch instrument; remote control is an opt-in convenience. Everything lives under Menu → MIDI Control, plus the dial button on the top strip.

1. Pick your controllers

Open Menu → MIDI Control and tick your controllers under Control Surfaces. It's a checklist, so several controllers can drive Dario at once — a keyboard, a fader box and a pad controller together. The list shows every MIDI source Dario can see and updates as you plug things in and out.

These messages drive on-screen controls only — they don't play the voices. To play a voice from a keyboard, use the SEQUENCE tab's EXT mode instead.

Routing is channel-aware, not device-specific. A mapping remembers the CC or note number and the channel it was learned on. If two controllers might clash on the same CC, put them on different MIDI channels.

Bluetooth controllers

For a wireless Bluetooth MIDI controller, open the menu's settings list and tap Bluetooth MIDI Devices (directly under MIDI Control Settings). This opens the system Bluetooth MIDI pairing list — pick your controller to pair it, allowing the Bluetooth permission the first time.

Once paired it behaves like any other MIDI device everywhere in Dario: in the Control Surfaces checklist, in the Clock Input picker, and as a MIDI Out destination. Bluetooth MIDI links sleep after a few idle minutes to save power and reconnect on the next activity — that's normal.

2. Mapping a control

There's a dial button on the top strip, between the padlock and the level meter. (An Arm MIDI Learn button in Menu → MIDI Control does the same thing.)

  1. Tap the dial to arm. A banner appears: "Tap any control to map it." The banner is unobtrusive — you can still reach the controls beneath it.
  2. Tap the Dario control you want to map. A card appears: "Move a knob, fader or pad on your controller…"
  3. Move the control on your controller. A MAP CONTROL card shows what it captured — the Dario control and the controller input, e.g. "POLY SYNTH · Cutoff ← CC 74 · Ch 1".
  4. Choose Save & Continue (keep it and go straight to the next control), Save & Finish, or Cancel.

So you arm once and map your whole controller in a single run.

3. What you can map

  • Voice & FX cards — the P sliders and B buttons, whether or not they're assigned to a plug-in parameter.
  • Mixer — each voice's Volume, Pan, FX 1 Send and FX 2 Send.
  • Bottom strip — per-voice MUTE / FADE / SOLO / ROLL, and the transport Play / Record / SS / MB.
  • Master column — the GAIN and PAN faders, and the SC and CLR buttons.
  • MODULATE — an LFO's shape (TYPE), RATE, RANGE, FADE and PHASE, and its ON/OFF, SYNC, BIPOLAR and GATE toggles.

4. How mapped controls behave

Sliders and faders follow the controller continuously, with a soft takeover so a hardware knob whose position doesn't match the screen doesn't make the value jump. The mode is a global setting in Menu → MIDI Control → Knob Takeover:

ModeBehaviour
Pick-Up (default)The value stays put until the knob passes through it, then follows. No jump, but there's a dead zone until you reach the value.
JumpThe value snaps to the knob immediately. Instant, but it can jump.
ScaleThe value moves proportionally toward the knob and meets it at the extremes. No jump and immediate; the mapping is momentarily non-linear until it converges.

Buttons follow the Dario button's own Toggle / Momentary setting — you decide the behaviour, not the controller. In Toggle, each controller press flips the button on and off; in Momentary, the button is on while the controller sends "on".

This is true whether the controller sends a Note or a CC, and whether its own button is momentary or latching. So the simplest setup is a momentary button on the controller, and Dario decides the rest — you never have to reconfigure the controller. A momentary press briefly lights the button so you can see it registered.

Transport and performance buttons do exactly what tapping them does: Play toggles play/stop, Record arms while playing, and MUTE / SOLO / FADE honour the Master Button latch.

5. Seeing your mappings — and conflicts

Menu → MIDI Control → MIDI Mappings lists every mapping: the Dario control on top and its controller input below. Each row has a trash button, and there's a Clear All Mappings at the bottom.

If the same controller input is mapped to more than one Dario control, Dario flags it with a warning triangle — on that row, and on the MAP CONTROL card while you're mapping. It's a warning, not a block: one knob controlling several things is sometimes exactly what you want.

6. Transport buttons that send MMC

Some control surfaces — the KORG nanoKONTROL2-style transport section, for example — send their Play / Stop / Record buttons as MMC (MIDI Machine Control) rather than a CC or note. Dario follows these automatically, with no mapping needed, since MMC is a fixed standard: Play starts, Stop stops, Record arms recording while playing.

A Follow MMC Transport switch in Menu → MIDI Control (on by default) turns this off for a device that sends stray MMC. It works alongside MIDI Learn — you can still learn a CC or note onto Play and Record as well.

Performance Lock and MIDI Learn

A mapped controller keeps working while the Performance Lock is on — locking the touchscreen against accidental changes doesn't stop your hardware driving the rig. Only re-mapping is affected: when the lock is blocking Change Parameters, MIDI Learn can't be armed and the dial dims. Existing mappings still work; you just can't change them until you unlock.

Why mappings are global

Your mappings, the ticked Control Surfaces and the Follow MMC setting are remembered app-wide and stay put as you switch Grooveboxes. They are not part of a Groovebox, so they don't travel with a shared .dario file.

Dario's controls are a fixed layout — Voice 1's P1 slider, MUTE, GAIN, an LFO's rate are in the same place in every Groovebox. So mapping "my knob → Voice 1's P1" is meaningful everywhere, and you set your controller up once. What that slider does changes with the Groovebox — it drives whatever P1 is assigned to — but the control your knob moves stays the same. If a Groovebox leaves a control unassigned, the mapping simply does nothing there, so a global map is never wrong.

This matches how a controller is a property of your rig, not of any one song — the same model hardware grooveboxes use. A per-song mapping would mean re-learning your whole controller in every Groovebox.

17Clock & Sync

Dario can lock its tempo and transport to the outside world — and drive other gear and apps in return — three ways: send MIDI Clock, follow MIDI Clock, and join an Ableton Link session.

Everything here lives in the menu: Clock & Sync Settings for the sync options, and Audio Engine Settings for Play in Background.

Clock Source — who owns the tempo

Clock Source picks the one thing that drives Dario's tempo and transport. The three choices are mutually exclusive, because two masters would fight:

  • Internal — Dario runs its own clock (the default). The BPM pill sets the tempo.
  • MIDI Clock In — Dario follows an external MIDI clock.
  • Ableton Link — Dario joins a Link session and shares tempo and beat with other Link apps and devices.

Sending MIDI Clock out is independent of this choice — you can send clock in any mode.

Send MIDI Clock — Dario as the master

Turn on Send MIDI Clock to make Dario a clock master. It sends standard MIDI Clock plus Start and Stop, so external gear and other apps can follow Dario's tempo and transport. There are two ways things receive it:

  • Other apps can subscribe to Dario directly — Dario appears to them as a MIDI source named "Dario".
  • Send Clock To — a checklist that appears when Send is on, listing every MIDI output on your device: other apps' inputs, and any external gear connected by USB, Bluetooth or network. Tick the ones you want. The list refreshes live, so a device you plug in while the menu is open appears right away.

The selection starts empty. Apps that subscribe to the "Dario" source still receive clock without being ticked. Pressing Play sends Start and Stop sends Stop, while the clock itself runs continuously so followers can pre-lock to the tempo.

MIDI Clock In — Dario as a follower

Set Clock Source to MIDI Clock In, then pick the master under Clock Input. Dario then follows the tempo — the BPM pill tracks the incoming clock, smoothed so it stays steady — and follows the transport, so the master's Start and Stop start and stop Dario in time.

Pick the source that's actually sending clock. A plain keyboard that only sends notes won't drive the tempo. Any device can be either a master or a follower, so the same one can appear in both the Clock Input list and the Send Clock To list.

Ableton Link

Ableton Link keeps tempo, beat position and start/stop in sync across Link-enabled apps and devices on the same local network — no cables, no setup. Set Clock Source to Ableton Link, open the Ableton Link settings row, and turn Link on. Other Link peers on the same Wi-Fi connect automatically.

  • Tempo is shared — change it anywhere and everyone follows.
  • Start/stop is shared — starting or stopping in one app starts or stops the others.
  • The bar lines up — when you start, Dario waits for the shared bar so its downbeat lands in phase with everyone else.
Two iPads, both running Dario. Dario announces itself to other copies of Dario on the network, not just to other Link apps — so a second iPad running Dario can join the same session and stay locked to the same tempo and bar.
Link needs the local network. The first time you use it, allow the local-network prompt; if you decline, Link can't find peers. The Link panel is Ableton's standard one, so it looks and works the same across every Link app, and it follows Dario's light or dark theme.

Play in Background

Audio Engine Settings → Play in Background decides whether Dario keeps making sound, and staying in sync, after you leave the app or lock the screen:

  • On — audio keeps running indefinitely, and Dario stays a Link / MIDI-clock peer while you're in another app. Best when you switch between Dario and another app while they play together.
  • 15 Minutes — audio keeps running for a grace period, then stops, so a session you wander away from doesn't run the battery down.
  • Off — audio stops as soon as you leave, saving the most battery.

Coming back to Dario always resumes audio — returning to the foreground reactivates the session, restarts the engine, and cancels a pending 15-minute teardown.

21Tips & Workflow

Set the Scale and Key first

It's global, and it's what guarantees four independent lanes never clash. Get it right up front and everything you sequence afterwards is in tune with everything else. If you're unsure, the Scale pill's GENRE / EMOTION tab will suggest a fitting combination in two taps.

Same progression, different STEPS

The single most productive trick in Dario. Give a pad and a bass the same progression but different LENGTH and DIV/MULT, and they move through the changes at different rates — interlocking, related, and consonant by construction. See 8.5.

Put a slow LFO on a generator parameter

An LFO on EUCLID PULSES, TURING CHANGE, or notes-EUCLID ROTATE makes a pattern that rewrites itself bar by bar while you work on something else. Because generator changes are bar-quantised, it never sounds like a glitch.

Map a button to an LFO's ON/OFF

The quickest performance move in the app: drop a wobble, a tremolo or a filter sweep in and out from the PERFORM tab without leaving it. Set the button to Momentary for hold-to-run.

Use SS before you experiment

Snapshot is free, instant, and captures the hosted plug-ins' internal state as well as Dario's own. Tap it before you start re-programming a hosted sequencer or re-dialling an effect, and you can always get back.

Rename your voices

Long-press a voice header and call it BASS or LEAD. The name follows into the assign pickers, the LFO card titles, the sequencer card headers and the LFO destination lists — which makes a complex rig far easier to navigate under pressure.

Build your controller map once

MIDI mappings are global and target fixed positions, so a map you build today keeps working across every Groovebox you ever load. Arm the dial once and map the whole controller in one run using Save & Continue.

Watch the CPU before you add the fourth plug-in

Hosting is expensive. The CPU read-out in the master strip is your early warning — check it after each plug-in you add, not after you hear crackling.

22Troubleshooting

A voice makes no sound

  • Check the voice has an instrument loaded in its top slot, and that its Vol isn't at zero.
  • Check MUTE isn't on for that voice, and that no other voice has SOLO engaged.
  • On the SEQUENCE tab, confirm the lane's note source matches where the notes are coming from: SEQ for the built-in sequencer, AUv3 for a hosted MIDI generator, EXT for a keyboard.
  • In DRUMS mode, remember the lane needs a hosted instrument to make sound — there's no fallback synth.
  • Check the lane's STEPS CHANNEL. If it's set to anything but 1 and the instrument only listens on channel 1, the notes arrive on a channel the plug-in ignores and you hear nothing. Set it back to 1, or set the plug-in to omni.
  • On a DRUMS lane, check that sound's STEPS NOTE too — the lane may be firing a note the plug-in has nothing mapped to. The button lights whenever a sound is off its General MIDI default.
  • If the voice's instrument is MIDI Out, it makes no audio by design.
  • If it's Audio Input or External Instrument, open its window and check it has channels ticked — an empty selection is silent — and that Menu → Audio Engine → Audio Input points at the interface your source is plugged into.

My FX send does nothing

An empty FX bus is silent — it isn't send-filled, rendered or summed. Load an effect into that bus and the send comes alive. A send to an empty bus is not a dry pass-through.

A slider won't move

  • An LFO is probably driving the same parameter. The slider shows a waveform badge and follows the LFO instead. Turn that LFO off to take back hands-on control — see 11.4.
  • Check whether a CLR button is armed: while it is, automated controls are temporarily locked so you can't nudge one by mistake.
  • Check the Performance Lock isn't armed with a scope that covers what you're trying to do.

I'm getting crackles or dropouts

  • Raise the Buffer Size in Menu → Audio Engine Settings. A larger buffer trades a little latency for a lot of stability.
  • Close plug-in windows you aren't using — every open window is live and drawing. Lower the Open Windows cap if you routinely have several open.
  • Bypass or remove the heaviest effect and watch the CPU read-out to find the culprit.
  • On an entry-level iPad, keep voice and effect counts modest. See 2. Requirements.

My external gear isn't receiving notes

  • Confirm the voice's instrument is the built-in MIDI Out or External Instrument, and that a Destination is selected in its window.
  • If the destination shows (offline), the device isn't connected — it reconnects automatically when it reappears.
  • Check the Channel: Pass-through sends each note on the channel it arrived on — for the built-in sequencer that's the lane's STEPS CHANNEL — which may not be what your synth is listening to. Pin it to a fixed 1–16 for a single mono/poly synth.
  • Remember MUTE and SOLO affect MIDI Out the same way they affect audio.

My hardware audio input isn't coming through

  • Check the input device in Menu → Audio Engine → Audio Input — it's global, shared by every input voice, and it's the first thing to get wrong. If no inputs are listed, connect the interface or allow microphone access in Settings › Dario.
  • Open the voice's window and confirm channels are ticked. Switching MODE between MONO and STEREO clears the selection, so re-pick after flipping it.
  • An orange note saying the device isn't connected means the Groovebox was saved with an interface that's now unplugged — reconnect it, or choose an available input.
  • An orange warning naming a different device means iOS aggregated your two interfaces and routed one of its own choosing. Try swapping the order you plug the USB cables in, then set Audio Output and Audio Input again; or patch the source into your main interface's inputs. See 14.5.
  • Remember the input voice is a normal voice — check its Vol, MUTE and whether another voice is soloed.

My controller isn't controlling anything

  • Tick it under Menu → MIDI Control → Control Surfaces. An unticked controller is ignored.
  • If a mapped fader seems dead at first, that's Pick-Up takeover — move it through the on-screen value and it engages. Switch to Jump or Scale in Knob Takeover if you'd rather it grab immediately.
  • Mappings are channel-aware: if you changed the controller's channel since learning, re-learn the mapping.
  • A controller can't play a voice from here — use the SEQUENCE tab's EXT mode for that.

A hosted plug-in came back empty after loading a Groovebox

Dario re-applies plug-in state over a short window to handle plug-ins that drop an early restore, so this should be rare. If a specific plug-in still comes back blank, try Reload on that slot. Note also that sampler-style plug-ins that reference sample files on the original device may not resolve those files on another iPad.

A lane sounds out of key

Check the global Scale isn't set to Chromatic, which deliberately disables snapping. Also check the lane's CHORD setting: the fixed "planing" shapes (MAJ, MIN, SUS4…) are allowed to leave the key by design. Switch to a diatonic voicing (DIA / DIA7 / DIA9) to stay in key — see 8.3.

My LFO isn't doing anything

  • LFOs are transport-locked — they only advance while the transport is playing. Press Play.
  • Check the card's ON / OFF button. When the LFO is off the card dims, but the ON/OFF button stays bright.
  • Check the target's DEPTH in the assign card — around 1 % is almost frozen.
  • A Sequencer target only does something when that voice's current generator actually has that control. GRIDS targets, for example, need the voice in DRUMS mode with GRIDS lanes.
